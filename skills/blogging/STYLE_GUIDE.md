# Chase McCoy's Writing Style Guide

## Voice & Tone

### Core Characteristics
- **Conversational yet thoughtful**: Approachable without being casual, intellectual without being academic
- **First-person perspective**: Uses "I" frequently to share personal experience and opinion
- **Inviting and inclusive**: Uses "you" to bring readers into the conversation
- **Curious and exploratory**: Asks questions, wonders aloud, explores ideas rather than declaring absolutes
- **Meta-aware**: Often reflects on the medium itself (web, design, writing)

### Tone Patterns
- Genuine enthusiasm for discoveries: "woah??" followed by "huh...?" followed by "a wide grin"
- Measured criticism without snark: concerns are explained, not dismissed
- Wonder and delight: "This is the kind of creative touch that makes designing for screens so special"
- Intellectual humility: "I'm not sure, but..." / "I wonder if..." / "Maybe..."

## Structure & Organization

### Opening Strategies
1. **Direct observation**: "Something I'm noticing about AI..."
2. **Personal statement**: "All designers have an origin story..."
3. **Surprising fact**: "Here's something unexpected: Keanu Reeves and China Miéville..."
4. **Setting context**: "I have a lot of nostalgia for the era of blogging..."
5. **Immediate enthusiasm**: "A good day on the web is one where..."

### Paragraph Characteristics
- **Short to medium length**: Rarely longer than 4-5 sentences
- **Single idea per paragraph**: Each paragraph advances one thought
- **White space**: Frequent paragraph breaks for readability
- **Rhythm variation**: Mix of short punchy paragraphs with longer explanatory ones

### Structural Elements
- Use of horizontal rules (`<hr class='break' />`) to mark major transitions
- Frequent use of block quotes with proper citation
- Embed videos with YouTube embeds: `https://www.youtube.com/embed/...`
- Screenshots and images with descriptive alt text and captions
- Code blocks when relevant, with language specification

### Post Length Patterns
- **Short posts** (100-200 words): Quick links or observations
- **Medium posts** (500-800 words): Most common, focused exploration
- **Long posts** (1000+ words): Deep dives, conference recaps, technical tutorials

## Sentence Mechanics

### Sentence Structure
- **Variety is key**: Mix of short, punchy sentences with longer, flowing ones
- **Start sentences varied ways**: Avoid repetitive patterns
- **Fragment sentences occasionally** for emphasis or rhythm: "Neat!" / "Pretty cool." / "Voila!"
- **Compound sentences** using em dashes, semicolons, colons for flow

### Punctuation Preferences

**Em dashes (—)**: Moderate use for parenthetical thoughts, expansions, or pivots
- "The specific knobs you can fiddle vary from tool to tool, but their existence and multitude are hard to avoid."
- Used frequently to add nuance or secondary thoughts

**Semicolons**: Used sparingly to connect closely related independent clauses
- Less common than em dashes or periods for connecting ideas

**Colons**: Moderate use for introducing elaborations or examples
- "Here's something that checks all kinds of boxes for me: Lori Emerson has a gorgeous new book..."
- Used to set up what follows

**Parentheticals**: Used sparingly but effectively
- "(and, in the future, the work created by Figma users, unless they opt out)"
- "(which I consider a net positive on the whole)"

**Question marks**: Frequent rhetorical questions to engage reader
- "How is a person who wants to effectively use the best available tools expected to possibly keep up?"
- "Will the same be true of our own data a hundred years from now?"

**Exclamation points**: Used genuinely for excitement, never ironically
- "Neat!" / "Pretty cool." / "Sign me up."

**Italics**: For emphasis, especially single words
- "_the_ most popular tool"
- "what a computer _could be_"
- "this is _because_ of AI"

**Bold**: Rarely used, mostly for headings or strong emphasis

**Small text**: Occasionally for asides
- `<small>(Sorry, I had to.)</small>`

**Mark/highlight**: For emphasized quotes
- `<mark>He is a poet of the mundane</mark>`

## Links & Citations

### Linking Patterns
- **Inline links** are the default: descriptive text is linked
- **Link to sources generously**: Every claim or reference gets a link
- **Descriptive link text**: Avoid "click here" - link meaningful phrases
- **Author attribution**: Link author names to their sites/profiles
- **Project links**: Always link to demos, examples, projects mentioned

### Citation Style
- Block quotes for longer excerpts

### External References
- Links to other writers' work, always with context
- Acknowledges influences and inspirations explicitly
- "I really enjoyed [this writeup from Matt Webb]..."
- "Jenny Wen recently wrote a great piece about this"

## Language & Word Choice

### Common Phrases & Verbal Tics
- "I was delighted to discover/see..."
- "I can't wait to [do/see/find out]..."
- "It's fascinating [that/how]..."
- "This is [neat/cool/lovely/fascinating]..."
- "I think..." (honest personal opinion marker)
- "Maybe..." (exploratory speculation)
- "Of course..." (acknowledging nuance)

### Technical Language
- **Accessible explanations**: Technical concepts explained clearly
- **Assumes intelligent reader**: Doesn't over-explain, trusts audience
- **Domain-specific terms** used naturally when appropriate
- **Abbreviations**: Spell out on first use, then abbreviate (MCP, LLM, etc.)

### Descriptive Language
- **Concrete imagery**: "blank canvases" / "wildlife preserves" / "flywheel gets spinning"
- **Metaphors from various domains**: Music, architecture, nature, tools
- **Sensory details**: "pale and pallid" / "ethereal brightness"
- **Evocative adjectives**: "ambitious", "delightful", "striking", "mundane"

## Content Patterns

### Topics & Themes
- Web technology and design
- AI and its implications
- Design systems and processes
- Internet culture and history
- Personal computing and data ownership
- Creative tools and workflows
- Music, books, art (personal interests)

### Balancing Act
- **Technical and personal**: Weaves technical analysis with personal reflection
- **Present and past**: Connects current trends to historical context
- **Enthusiasm and criticism**: Excited about potential while acknowledging concerns
- **Concrete and abstract**: Specific examples ground abstract concepts

### Idea Development
1. Introduce observation or discovery
2. Provide context and background
3. Explore implications or connections
4. Share personal perspective
5. Sometimes end with open question or call to action

### Endings
- **Open-ended**: Often ends with questions or possibilities rather than conclusions
- **Forward-looking**: "I can't wait to find out" / "I'm excited about..."
- **Invitation**: "I encourage you to..." / "Check it out..."
- **Reflection**: Circles back to main theme with new perspective
- **Sometimes abrupt**: Short posts end quickly without elaborate conclusions

## Formatting Conventions

### Special Elements
- Horizontal rules for section breaks: `<hr class='break' />`
- Image captions in quotes after image: `![](image.png "Caption text")`
- Embedded videos via YouTube iframe embeds

## Don'ts

- **No internet slang** or meme language
- **No forced humor** or jokes
- **No clickbait** or sensationalism
- **No listicles** without substance
- **No SEO-optimized** headline patterns
- **No excessive self-promotion**

## Do's

- **Be genuine** in enthusiasm and criticism
- **Credit sources** generously
- **Ask questions** to engage readers
- **Share personal experience** when relevant
- **Connect ideas** across domains
- **Embrace nuance** and complexity
- **Edit ruthlessly** for clarity and concision
- **Link extensively** to provide context
- **Use examples** to ground abstract ideas
- **End naturally** without forced conclusions

## Meta Notes

### Influences (Based on references)
- Jason Kottke (blogging form)
- Robin Sloan (thoughtful technology writing)

### Philosophy
The writing reflects a belief that:
- The medium shapes the message
- Personal websites are spaces for exploration
- Writing is thinking
- The web is malleable and should remain so
- Technology should serve humans, not vice versa
- Good design is about solving problems, not aesthetics alone
